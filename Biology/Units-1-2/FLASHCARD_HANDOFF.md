## Purpose and study strategy

Build the smallest practical deck that still gives reliable coverage of the VCAA VCE Biology Units 1–2 key knowledge and transferable exam skills.

- Prioritise active recall, application and common misconceptions over long textbook summaries.
- One assessable idea per card; combine tightly related facts where that reduces repetition without making the answer hard to recall.
- Keep answers concise (ideally one or two sentences).
- Do not create a separate card for every tiny vocabulary term.
- Use scenario-based cards for applying concepts, especially Unit 2 AOS3.
- Avoid duplicating cards already in the deck. When adding cards, check this handoff first.
- Use the current VCAA Biology Study Design 2022–2026 as the baseline, but verify the school’s prescribed study design if the course year changes.

## Learner profile and priorities

- Learner reports being reasonably confident with most Unit 1 and Unit 2 content.
- Main weakness: **Unit 2 AOS3 — contemporary bioethical issues**.
- Give AOS3 disproportionate practice, but do not neglect the core content.
- Explain difficult ideas with simple analogies when teaching; keep flashcard answers exam-appropriate.
- Goal: efficient revision in the shortest practical time, not a huge deck.

## Topic coverage map

### Unit 1 — How do organisms regulate their functions?

**AOS1: How do cells function?**
- Cell theory; prokaryotic/eukaryotic and plant/animal cell structures.
- Organelles and the relationship between structure and function.
- Surface-area-to-volume ratio and limits on cell size.
- Plasma membrane structure and selective permeability.
- Diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion and active transport.
- Cell cycle, mitosis, cytokinesis, apoptosis and cell-cycle regulation.
- Stem cells, potency, differentiation and specialisation.

**AOS2: How do plant and animal systems function?**
- Plant cell specialisation and organisation into tissues/organs.
- Water uptake, xylem transport, transpiration and stomatal regulation.
- Animal cell specialisation and organisation into tissues/organs/systems.
- Digestive, endocrine and excretory system structures and functions.
- Homeostasis and stimulus–response models.
- Feedback loops and regulation of body temperature, blood glucose and water balance.
- Malfunctions: type 1 diabetes, hypoglycaemia and hyperthyroidism.

**AOS3: How is scientific inquiry used to investigate cellular and system function?**
- Research question, aim, hypothesis and prediction.
- Variables, controls, sampling and methodology.
- Data collection, processing, graphing and interpretation.
- Accuracy, precision, repeatability, reproducibility and validity.
- Random/systematic errors, uncertainty, outliers and limitations.
- Evidence-based conclusions and scientific communication.

### Unit 2 — How does inheritance impact on diversity?

**AOS1: How is inheritance explained?**
- DNA, genes, alleles, genomes, loci and homologous chromosomes.
- Autosomes, sex chromosomes, haploid/diploid cells and karyotypes.
- Meiosis, crossing over, independent assortment and fertilisation.
- Genotype, phenotype, dominant/recessive alleles, codominance and incomplete dominance.
- Genetic, environmental and epigenetic influences on phenotype.
- Monohybrid crosses, test crosses, pedigrees and sex-linked inheritance.
- Linked genes versus independent assortment; two-gene crosses.

**AOS2: How do inherited adaptations impact on diversity?**
- Asexual versus sexual reproduction; advantages and disadvantages.
- Reproductive cloning: process, applications and implications.
- Genetic diversity and population resilience.
- Structural, physiological and behavioural adaptations.
- Species interdependence, predators, keystone species and population distribution/density/size.
- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples’ contributions to ecological knowledge and understanding.

**AOS3: How is scientific inquiry used to investigate contemporary bioethical issues?**
- Primary versus secondary research; evidence versus opinion/anecdote.
- Source credibility, authority, validity, reliability, relevance and bias.
- Organising, analysing and synthesising secondary data.
- Scientific models/representations and their limitations.
- Ethical approaches: consequences-based, duty/rule-based and virtue-based.
- Ethical concepts: integrity, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence and respect.
- Stakeholders and social, economic, legal and political factors.
- Justified position, communication and referencing.

## What is missing / next cards to create

Do **not** add more generic definition cards yet. The current deck has a basic AOS3 vocabulary framework, but it does not adequately train the learner to apply it to unfamiliar contemporary issues.

### Next priority: Unit 2 AOS3 application mini-deck

Create approximately 10–12 high-value cards, in this order:

1. A reusable step-by-step framework for analysing an unfamiliar bioethical scenario.
2. Distinguish scientific question from ethical question.
3. Analyse stakeholders, benefits, harms and competing interests.
4. Apply consequences-based reasoning without simply listing pros and cons.
5. Apply duty/rule-based reasoning (rights, consent, privacy, obligations).
6. Apply virtue-based reasoning (character, integrity, compassion, responsibility).
7. Distinguish an ethical principle from an ethical approach.
8. Evaluate quality and limitations of conflicting secondary sources.
9. Explain how uncertainty, bias and missing evidence affect a conclusion.
10. Build a balanced, justified conclusion that acknowledges a counterargument.
11. Apply the framework to a genetic testing scenario.
12. Apply the framework to a cloning or biodiversity-conservation scenario.

Use varied scenarios, not just memorised examples. Cards should require an action or judgement, not merely repeat a definition.
