## 1. How to use this deck

**Goal:** the smallest useful deck that prepares the learner to retrieve, explain and apply the examinable content. This is not a textbook summary.

Flashcard design rules:
- Each front asks one clear, answerable question or sets one specific task.
- Each back gives the minimum complete answer: precise enough for marks, short enough to retrieve quickly.
- Use comparison cards when the distinction is commonly confused.
- Use process cards for sequences and application cards for interpreting data/scenarios.
- Avoid vague prompts (“Explain everything about…”) and answers that are just unexplained keywords.
- Avoid duplicate cards. If a card tests two ideas that can be recalled separately, split it; if two facts are inseparable, combine them.
- Do not add content simply because it appears in the wider study design. The teacher checklist defines this exam's scope.
- After learning a card, practise answering without looking. Mark it “secure” only after recalling it correctly on separate reviews. Revisit missed cards more often.

## 2. Learner priorities

- Learner reports being reasonably confident with most topics.
- Main difficulty: **bioethics — applying ethical approaches and principles**, not merely defining them.
- High-priority practice: bioethical scenarios, gel electrophoresis interpretation, pedigrees/monohybrid crosses, graph analysis.
- Explain difficult concepts with simple analogies during teaching, but keep the card answers scientifically accurate and exam-ready.
- Do not assume a topic is mastered just because it has a card; use short retrieval checks.

# 6. Active flashcard deck

Cards are grouped by topic for navigation, but should be shuffled during retrieval practice so the learner must identify the relevant concept independently.

## A. Cells and membranes

### A01 — Prokaryotic vs eukaryotic cells
**Front:** What is the key structural difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?  
**Back:** Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; their DNA is in a nucleoid region. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

### A02 — Plant vs animal cells
**Front:** Name the structures present in plant cells but not typical animal cells, and state their functions.  
**Back:** Cellulose cell wall: support and shape; chloroplasts: photosynthesis; large permanent vacuole: stores cell sap and helps maintain turgor. Both plant and animal cells have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria and ribosomes.

### A03 — Organelle functions
**Front:** Match each organelle to its main function: nucleus, ribosome, mitochondrion, rough ER, smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosome, chloroplast.  
**Back:** Nucleus: stores DNA/controls gene expression; ribosome: protein synthesis; mitochondrion: aerobic respiration/ATP production; rough ER: synthesis/transport of proteins; smooth ER: lipid synthesis; Golgi: modifies, sorts and packages molecules; lysosome: intracellular digestion; chloroplast: photosynthesis.

### A04 — Surface-area-to-volume ratio
**Front:** Why does a high surface-area-to-volume ratio help a cell exchange materials?  
**Back:** The membrane surface available for exchange is large relative to the cell's volume and demand. As a cell gets larger, volume increases faster than surface area, making exchange less efficient.

### A05 — Plasma membrane structure
**Front:** Describe the structure of the plasma membrane and explain how it is selectively permeable.  
**Back:** It is a fluid phospholipid bilayer with hydrophilic heads facing watery environments and hydrophobic tails facing inward, with embedded proteins. The hydrophobic core restricts many polar/charged substances, while specific proteins allow selected substances to cross.

### A06 — Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
**Front:** Distinguish diffusion, osmosis and active transport.  
**Back:** Diffusion is net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration. Osmosis is net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from higher water concentration to lower water concentration. Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient using energy and transport proteins.

### A07 — Bulk transport
**Front:** Compare endocytosis, pinocytosis and exocytosis.  
**Back:** Endocytosis brings material into a cell when the membrane encloses it in a vesicle. Pinocytosis is endocytosis of extracellular fluid and dissolved substances. Exocytosis releases material when a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane. These are energy-requiring bulk-transport processes.

## B. Stem cells and cell division

### B01 — Stem-cell properties
**Front:** What two properties define a stem cell?  
**Back:** Self-renewal: it can divide to produce more stem cells. Potency/differentiation potential: it can develop into one or more specialised cell types.

### B02 — Stem-cell potency
**Front:** Distinguish totipotent, pluripotent and multipotent stem cells.  
**Back:** Totipotent cells can form all cell types, including extra-embryonic tissues; pluripotent cells can form almost all body cell types; multipotent cells can form a more limited range of related cell types.

### B03 — Cell cycle
**Front:** What happens during G1, S, G2 and M phases of the cell cycle?  
**Back:** G1: cell grows and carries out normal functions; S: DNA is replicated; G2: further growth and preparation for division; M: mitosis separates duplicated chromosomes and cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.

### B04 — Apoptosis
**Front:** What is apoptosis, and why is it important?  
**Back:** Apoptosis is controlled, programmed cell death. It removes unnecessary, damaged or potentially harmful cells in an orderly way.

### B05 — Mitosis
**Front:** What is the outcome and purpose of mitosis?  
**Back:** One parent cell produces two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number. It supports growth, repair and cell replacement.

### B06 — Meiosis
**Front:** What is the outcome and purpose of meiosis?  
**Back:** One diploid cell undergoes two divisions to produce four haploid cells that are genetically varied. It produces gametes for sexual reproduction.

### B07 — Mitosis vs meiosis
**Front:** Give three differences between mitosis and meiosis.  
**Back:** Mitosis has one division; meiosis has two. Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells; meiosis produces four genetically varied cells. Mitosis maintains chromosome number; meiosis halves it.

## C. Organ systems in the exam scope

### C01 — Digestion
**Front:** What is the difference between mechanical and chemical digestion?  
**Back:** Mechanical digestion physically breaks food into smaller pieces without changing its chemical composition. Chemical digestion uses enzymes to break large food molecules into smaller soluble molecules that can be absorbed.

### C02 — Digestive tract and absorption
**Front:** Where does most nutrient absorption occur, and how do nutrients enter the body?  
**Back:** Most nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine. Digested small molecules cross the intestinal lining; many enter blood capillaries, while most absorbed lipids enter lymph vessels.

### C03 — Enzyme specificity
**Front:** Why does each digestive enzyme act on particular substrates?  
**Back:** An enzyme's active site has a shape and chemical properties complementary to its substrate, so only particular substrates bind effectively.

### C04 — Endocrine communication
**Front:** How does the endocrine system send a message to a target cell?  
**Back:** An endocrine gland releases a hormone into the bloodstream. The hormone affects target cells that have the matching receptor.

### C05 — Insulin and glucagon
**Front:** How do insulin and glucagon have opposite effects on blood glucose?  
**Back:** When blood glucose is high, insulin promotes glucose uptake and storage, lowering blood glucose. When blood glucose is low, glucagon promotes the release of glucose into the blood, raising it.

### C06 — Diabetes
**Front:** What is diabetes mellitus, and how does type 1 differ from type 2?  
**Back:** Diabetes mellitus involves blood glucose remaining abnormally high because insulin production or action is inadequate. Type 1 involves destruction of pancreatic beta cells and little or no insulin production; type 2 involves body cells becoming less responsive to insulin, often with insufficient insulin production over time.

### C07 — Plant transport structures
**Front:** Distinguish the functions of root hair cells, xylem and phloem.  
**Back:** Root hair cells increase root surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions. Xylem transports water and mineral ions through the plant. Phloem transports sugars and other organic substances.

## D. Reproduction, adaptations and ecosystems

### D01 — Asexual vs sexual reproduction
**Front:** Compare asexual and sexual reproduction in terms of parents, gametes and genetic variation.  
**Back:** Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no fusion of gametes; offspring are usually genetically very similar. Sexual reproduction involves fusion of gametes and produces genetically varied offspring.

### D02 — Cloning
**Front:** What is cloning, and what does it mean for the genetic similarity of the clone?  
**Back:** Cloning produces a genetically near-identical copy of a cell or organism. A clone has essentially the same nuclear DNA as the source, although environmental effects and mutations can cause differences.

### D03 — Adaptation types
**Front:** Distinguish structural, behavioural and physiological adaptations.  
**Back:** Structural: a physical feature (e.g. thick fur). Behavioural: an action (e.g. nocturnal activity). Physiological: an internal body process (e.g. producing concentrated urine). Explain how the example improves survival or reproduction in its environment.

### D04 — Species interactions
**Front:** For each interaction, state which species benefits or is harmed: mutualism, commensalism, parasitism and predation.  
**Back:** Mutualism (+/+): both benefit. Commensalism (+/0): one benefits, the other is not significantly affected. Parasitism (+/−): parasite benefits, host is harmed. Predation (+/−): predator captures and consumes prey.

### D05 — Food chains and food webs
**Front:** How do food chains and food webs differ, and which way do arrows point?  
**Back:** A food chain shows one pathway of feeding/energy transfer; a food web shows interconnected feeding pathways. Arrows point from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it, showing energy transfer.

### D06 — Keystone species
**Front:** What is a keystone species, and what may happen if it is removed?  
**Back:** A keystone species has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem relative to its abundance. Its removal can cause major changes in populations and food-web structure.

## E. Genetics and inheritance

### E01 — Chromosome, gene and allele
**Front:** Distinguish a chromosome, a gene and an allele.  
**Back:** A chromosome is a DNA molecule packaged with proteins. A gene is a DNA sequence at a particular locus. An allele is an alternative version of a gene.

### E02 — Autosomes vs sex chromosomes
**Front:** What is the difference between autosomes and sex chromosomes?  
**Back:** Autosomes are chromosomes not classified as sex chromosomes. Sex chromosomes are involved in sex determination in many organisms and may carry genes related to sex-linked traits.

### E03 — Chromosome variation and karyotypes
**Front:** What information can a karyotype show?  
**Back:** A karyotype displays an individual's chromosomes arranged in pairs, usually by size, banding pattern and centromere position. It can help identify chromosome-number or large structural differences and show the sex-chromosome complement.

### E04 — Genotype and phenotype
**Front:** Distinguish genotype and phenotype.  
**Back:** Genotype is the allele combination an organism has. Phenotype is its observable characteristic, resulting from the expression of genes (and, where relevant, environmental influences).

### E05 — Dominant and recessive
**Front:** In a simple complete-dominance pattern, how do dominant and recessive alleles affect phenotype?  
**Back:** One copy of a dominant allele is sufficient for the dominant phenotype. The recessive phenotype appears only when no dominant allele is present (typically homozygous recessive).

### E06 — Codominance vs incomplete dominance
**Front:** Distinguish codominance from incomplete dominance in a heterozygote.  
**Back:** In codominance, both alleles are fully expressed in the phenotype. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.

### E07 — Monohybrid Punnett square
**Front:** What steps do you follow to predict offspring from a monohybrid cross?  
**Back:** 1. Define allele symbols and parental genotypes. 2. List the gametes each parent can produce. 3. Combine gametes in a Punnett square. 4. Count genotype and phenotype outcomes. 5. State probabilities or ratios, not guaranteed numbers of offspring.

### E08 — Pedigree interpretation
**Front:** What clues can help distinguish dominant, recessive, autosomal and sex-linked inheritance in a pedigree?  
**Back:** Dominant traits often appear in successive generations; recessive traits can skip generations and appear in children of unaffected carrier parents. For sex-linked patterns, check whether the trait occurs differently in males/females and whether father-to-son transmission is possible (it is not for X-linked traits). Use the whole pedigree; one clue alone is not proof.

## F. Scientific skills and gel electrophoresis

### F01 — Experimental variables
**Front:** In an experiment, define the independent, dependent and controlled variables.  
**Back:** Independent variable: deliberately changed. Dependent variable: measured outcome. Controlled variables: factors kept constant so they do not provide alternative explanations for the result.

### F02 — Graph analysis
**Front:** What should a strong answer include when describing a graph or data trend?  
**Back:** Identify the variables and units; describe the overall trend; support it with specific data values; compare relevant groups; identify anomalies if present; and make a conclusion that answers the question without claiming more than the data show.

### F03 — Gel electrophoresis process
**Front:** Describe the basic process of DNA gel electrophoresis.  
**Back:** DNA samples and a DNA size ladder are loaded into wells in a gel. An electric field is applied; negatively charged DNA moves towards the positive electrode. The gel separates fragments by size, and bands are visualised for comparison.

### F04 — Gel electrophoresis interpretation
**Front:** How do you interpret DNA bands in a gel?  
**Back:** Smaller DNA fragments generally travel farther through the gel than larger fragments. Compare each sample's band positions with the size ladder to estimate fragment sizes; samples with matching band patterns have fragments of corresponding lengths. A band pattern alone does not automatically prove identity.

## G. Bioethics — highest-priority application practice

### G01 — Ethical approaches
**Front:** Apply the three ethical approaches to one biological decision. What question does each approach ask?  
**Back:** Consequences-based: which option produces the best overall balance of benefits and harms? Duty/rule-based: what rights, duties, rules or obligations must be respected? Virtue-based: what would a person acting with good character and values do? Apply each to the scenario's facts rather than just naming it.

### G02 — Ethical principles
**Front:** Define beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, respect and integrity.  
**Back:** Beneficence: act to promote good/wellbeing. Non-maleficence: avoid or minimise harm. Justice: treat people fairly and distribute benefits/risks fairly. Respect: recognise dignity, autonomy and relevant rights. Integrity: act honestly and responsibly.

### G03 — Approach vs principle
**Front:** What is the difference between an ethical approach and an ethical principle?  
**Back:** An approach is a way of reasoning through a decision (consequences-, duty/rule- or virtue-based). A principle is a value or standard used to judge conduct (e.g. justice or respect). A principle can be considered within more than one approach.

### G04 — Bioethical scenario method
**Front:** You are given an unfamiliar bioethical scenario. What steps should your answer follow?  
**Back:** 1. State the biological decision/issue. 2. Identify the stakeholders. 3. Identify the competing benefits, harms, rights or responsibilities. 4. Apply the named ethical approach/principle to specific scenario details. 5. Give a justified judgement and acknowledge a relevant counterconsideration.

### G05 — Apply an approach
**Front:** A genetic test could help a person make health decisions, but the result may reveal private information about relatives. Give one consequences-based and one duty-based consideration.  
**Back:** Consequences-based: weigh the health benefits and possible harms such as anxiety, discrimination or family conflict for everyone affected. Duty-based: consider informed consent, confidentiality and the person's rights over their genetic information. A good answer links each point to the facts; it does not assume the outcome.

### G06 — Justified ethical conclusion
**Front:** What makes a short bioethical conclusion well justified?  
**Back:** It clearly states a position, supports it with relevant biological facts and ethical reasoning, considers who is affected, and recognises at least one important limitation or competing consideration. It does not present personal opinion as scientific evidence.
