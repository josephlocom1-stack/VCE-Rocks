## Flashcard rules

- Each front tests one clear thing.
- Each back uses **plain English but keeps the scientific keywords** needed for marks.
- Define a technical term briefly when it first appears.
- Use short bullets, steps or comparisons where they make recall easier.
- No padding, vague wording, duplicate cards or unnecessary textbook detail.
- Keep the exact scientific meaning; simpler wording must not make the answer inaccurate.
- Analogy can help explain a concept during teaching, but the exam answer must still use the correct biological term.
- Cards are grouped below for learning; shuffle them for testing.
- A card is not mastered just because it has been read. Recall it correctly on separate reviews.

# Active flashcard deck

## A. Cells and membranes

### A01 — Prokaryotic vs eukaryotic cells
**Front:** What is the main difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?  
**Back:**
- **Prokaryotic:** no membrane-bound nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; DNA is in the cytoplasm (nucleoid region).
- **Eukaryotic:** has a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

### A02 — Plant vs animal cells
**Front:** What three structures are found in typical plant cells but not animal cells? Give each function.  
**Back:**
- **Cellulose cell wall:** supports and strengthens the cell.
- **Chloroplasts:** carry out photosynthesis.
- **Large permanent vacuole:** stores cell sap and helps keep the cell firm (turgid).
Both types have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria and ribosomes.

### A03 — Organelle functions
**Front:** What is the main job of each organelle: nucleus, ribosome, mitochondrion, rough ER, smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosome and chloroplast?  
**Back:**
- **Nucleus:** contains DNA; controls cell activities through gene expression.
- **Ribosome:** makes proteins.
- **Mitochondrion:** site of aerobic respiration; releases energy as ATP.
- **Rough ER:** makes and transports proteins (has ribosomes).
- **Smooth ER:** makes lipids.
- **Golgi apparatus:** modifies, sorts and packages molecules.
- **Lysosome:** breaks down materials using digestive enzymes.
- **Chloroplast:** site of photosynthesis.

### A04 — Surface-area-to-volume ratio
**Front:** Why are small cells better at exchanging materials than very large cells?  
**Back:** A small cell has more **surface area compared with its volume**. This gives it more membrane for exchanging materials relative to the amount of cell that needs supplies. As a cell grows, volume increases faster than surface area, so exchange becomes less efficient.

### A05 — Plasma membrane
**Front:** Describe the plasma membrane structure and explain selective permeability.  
**Back:**
- It is a **phospholipid bilayer**: water-attracting (**hydrophilic**) heads face the watery surroundings; water-repelling (**hydrophobic**) tails face inwards.
- It contains membrane proteins.
- It is **selectively permeable**: some substances cross easily, while others need specific proteins or cannot cross.
The hydrophobic middle blocks many charged or water-soluble substances.

### A06 — Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
**Front:** How are diffusion, osmosis and active transport different?  
**Back:**
- **Diffusion:** particles spread from high to low concentration.
- **Osmosis:** water moves across a selectively permeable membrane from higher water concentration to lower water concentration.
- **Active transport:** membrane proteins use energy (ATP) to move substances against their concentration gradient, from low to high concentration.
Think: diffusion is spreading downhill; active transport is pushing uphill.

### A07 — Bulk transport
**Front:** Compare endocytosis, pinocytosis and exocytosis.  
**Back:**
- **Endocytosis:** the cell membrane folds in and forms a vesicle to bring material into the cell.
- **Pinocytosis:** a type of endocytosis that takes in fluid and dissolved substances (“cell drinking”).
- **Exocytosis:** a vesicle joins the cell membrane and releases its contents outside the cell.
These processes move larger amounts/materials using vesicles and energy.

## B. Stem cells and cell division

### B01 — Stem-cell properties
**Front:** What two abilities make a cell a stem cell?  
**Back:**
- **Self-renewal:** can divide to make more stem cells.
- **Potency/differentiation:** can develop into specialised cell types.

### B02 — Stem-cell potency
**Front:** What is the difference between totipotent, pluripotent and multipotent stem cells?  
**Back:**
- **Totipotent:** can form all cell types, including extra-embryonic tissues.
- **Pluripotent:** can form almost all body cell types, but not a whole organism on their own.
- **Multipotent:** can form a limited range of related cell types.
Memory cue: total range → almost all → limited group.

### B03 — Cell cycle
**Front:** What happens in the G1, S, G2 and M stages of the cell cycle?  
**Back:**
- **G1:** cell grows and carries out normal jobs.
- **S:** DNA is copied (replicated).
- **G2:** cell grows more and prepares to divide.
- **M:** mitosis separates chromosomes; cytokinesis splits the cytoplasm to form two cells.

### B04 — Apoptosis
**Front:** What is apoptosis, and why does the body use it?  
**Back:** **Apoptosis** is controlled or programmed cell death. It safely removes cells that are no longer needed or are damaged, helping tissues develop and stay healthy.

### B05 — Mitosis
**Front:** What does mitosis produce, and why is it useful?  
**Back:** One parent cell produces **two genetically identical daughter cells** with the same chromosome number. It is used for growth, repair and replacing cells.

### B06 — Meiosis
**Front:** What does meiosis produce, and why is it needed?  
**Back:** One diploid cell divides twice to produce **four haploid cells** (half the original chromosome number). These become gametes for sexual reproduction.

### B07 — Mitosis vs meiosis
**Front:** Give three key differences between mitosis and meiosis.  
**Back:**
- **Divisions:** mitosis = one; meiosis = two.
- **Products:** mitosis = two genetically identical cells; meiosis = four genetically varied cells.
- **Chromosome number:** mitosis keeps it the same; meiosis halves it.

## C. Organ systems in the exam scope

### C01 — Mechanical vs chemical digestion
**Front:** What is the difference between mechanical and chemical digestion?  
**Back:**
- **Mechanical digestion:** physically breaks food into smaller pieces (e.g. chewing); food molecules are not chemically changed.
- **Chemical digestion:** enzymes break large food molecules into smaller, soluble molecules that can be absorbed.

### C02 — Nutrient absorption
**Front:** Where does most nutrient absorption happen, and where do absorbed nutrients go?  
**Back:** Most absorption happens in the **small intestine**. Small digested molecules pass through its lining. Most enter blood capillaries; most absorbed fats enter lymph vessels.

### C03 — Enzyme specificity
**Front:** Why does a digestive enzyme only work on particular substrates?  
**Back:** The enzyme's **active site** has a particular shape and chemical properties. Only a matching **substrate** can bind properly and be acted on. Think of a matching key and lock.

### C04 — Endocrine system
**Front:** How does a hormone from an endocrine gland affect a target cell?  
**Back:**
1. An **endocrine gland** releases a **hormone** into the blood.
2. The blood carries it around the body.
3. Only target cells with the matching **receptor** respond.
Analogy: the hormone is a message; the receptor is the correct receiver.

### C05 — Insulin vs glucagon
**Front:** How do insulin and glucagon control blood glucose in opposite ways?  
**Back:**
- When blood glucose is **too high**, the pancreas releases **insulin**. Cells take up more glucose and the liver stores glucose as glycogen, so blood glucose falls.
- When blood glucose is **too low**, the pancreas releases **glucagon**. The liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose, so blood glucose rises.
They help keep blood glucose within a normal range.

### C06 — Diabetes
**Front:** What is diabetes mellitus? How do type 1 and type 2 differ?  
**Back:**
- **Diabetes mellitus:** blood glucose stays too high because there is not enough effective insulin.
- **Type 1:** the immune system destroys pancreatic beta cells, so little or no insulin is made.
- **Type 2:** body cells become less responsive to insulin (**insulin resistance**); over time, the body may also fail to make enough insulin.

### C07 — Plant transport structures
**Front:** What do root hair cells, xylem and phloem each do?  
**Back:**
- **Root hair cells:** their long extensions give a large surface area to absorb water and mineral ions from soil.
- **Xylem:** carries water and mineral ions from roots through the plant.
- **Phloem:** carries sugars (such as sucrose) and other dissolved organic substances around the plant.

## D. Reproduction, adaptations and ecosystems

### D01 — Asexual vs sexual reproduction
**Front:** Compare asexual and sexual reproduction: number of parents, gametes and genetic variation.  
**Back:**
- **Asexual:** one parent; no fusion of gametes; offspring are usually genetically identical to the parent (clones).
- **Sexual:** gametes fuse; offspring inherit genetic material from two gametes and are genetically varied.

### D02 — Cloning
**Front:** What is cloning, and how genetically similar is a clone to its source?  
**Back:** **Cloning** makes a genetically near-identical copy of a cell or organism. An organism cloned from another usually has the same nuclear DNA, but may still differ because of mutations or environmental effects.

### D03 — Types of adaptation
**Front:** What is a structural, behavioural and physiological adaptation?  
**Back:**
- **Structural:** a physical body feature (e.g. thick fur).
- **Behavioural:** an action (e.g. being active at night).
- **Physiological:** an internal body process (e.g. producing a toxin).
An adaptation helps an organism survive and/or reproduce in its environment.

### D04 — Species interactions
**Front:** In mutualism, commensalism, parasitism and predation, who benefits or is harmed?  
**Back:**
- **Mutualism (+/+):** both species benefit.
- **Commensalism (+/0):** one benefits; the other is not significantly affected.
- **Parasitism (+/−):** parasite benefits; host is harmed.
- **Predation (+/−):** predator benefits by capturing/eating prey; prey is harmed/killed.

### D05 — Food chains and food webs
**Front:** How is a food web different from a food chain, and which way do arrows point?  
**Back:**
- **Food chain:** one pathway showing who eats whom.
- **Food web:** many connected food chains.
- Arrows point **from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it**. They show the direction of energy transfer.

### D06 — Keystone species
**Front:** What is a keystone species, and what might happen if it disappears?  
**Back:** A **keystone species** has a much bigger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers might suggest. Removing it can cause major changes across the food web, because other populations depend on it directly or indirectly.

## E. Genetics and inheritance

### E01 — Chromosome, gene and allele
**Front:** What is the difference between a chromosome, a gene and an allele?  
**Back:**
- **Chromosome:** a long DNA molecule packaged with proteins.
- **Gene:** a section of DNA that contains instructions for a functional product and can influence a characteristic.
- **Allele:** a different version of the same gene.

### E02 — Autosomes vs sex chromosomes
**Front:** What are autosomes and sex chromosomes?  
**Back:**
- **Autosomes:** all chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes.
- **Sex chromosomes:** chromosomes involved in determining biological sex in many organisms. They can also carry genes for other characteristics, including sex-linked traits.

### E03 — Karyotypes
**Front:** What is a karyotype, and what can it help you spot?  
**Back:** A **karyotype** is a picture of an individual's chromosomes arranged into pairs by features such as size and shape. It can show chromosome-number changes (extra or missing chromosomes) and some large structural changes.

### E04 — Genotype vs phenotype
**Front:** What is genotype, and what is phenotype?  
**Back:**
- **Genotype:** the allele combination an organism has.
- **Phenotype:** the characteristic you can observe.
Phenotype is affected by the genotype and, for some traits, the environment.

### E05 — Dominant vs recessive
**Front:** With complete dominance, when does a dominant or recessive phenotype appear?  
**Back:**
- **Dominant phenotype:** appears if at least one dominant allele is present (e.g. AA or Aa).
- **Recessive phenotype:** appears only with two recessive alleles (aa).
This rule applies to simple complete-dominance questions.

### E06 — Codominance vs incomplete dominance
**Front:** What does a heterozygote look like in codominance compared with incomplete dominance?  
**Back:**
- **Codominance:** both alleles are clearly expressed (both traits show).
- **Incomplete dominance:** the heterozygote has an intermediate or blended phenotype.
Key difference: both show vs intermediate.

### E07 — Monohybrid Punnett square
**Front:** What steps do you use to solve a monohybrid cross?  
**Back:**
1. Write the parents' **genotypes**.
2. Work out the **gametes** each parent can make (one allele per gamete).
3. Fill in the Punnett square.
4. Count possible **genotypes** and **phenotypes**.
5. Give the ratio or probability. It predicts chances, not the guaranteed outcome of a small family.

### E08 — Pedigrees
**Front:** What clues help identify dominant, recessive and X-linked inheritance in a pedigree?  
**Back:**
- **Dominant:** often appears in each generation; an affected person usually has an affected parent.
- **Recessive:** can skip generations; two unaffected carrier parents can have an affected child.
- **X-linked:** no father-to-son transmission, because fathers give sons a Y chromosome, not their X chromosome. Check the pattern across the whole family; one clue alone may not prove the inheritance type.

## F. Science skills and gel electrophoresis

### F01 — Variables
**Front:** What are the independent, dependent and controlled variables in an experiment?  
**Back:**
- **Independent variable (IV):** what the researcher deliberately changes.
- **Dependent variable (DV):** what the researcher measures.
- **Controlled variables:** factors kept the same to make the test fair.

### F02 — Graph and data analysis
**Front:** What should you include when describing a graph in an exam?  
**Back:**
1. Name the variables and units.
2. State the main trend or pattern.
3. Use actual numbers from the graph as evidence.
4. Compare groups if relevant.
5. Mention unusual results if present.
6. Answer the question without claiming more than the data show.

### F03 — Gel electrophoresis process
**Front:** How does DNA gel electrophoresis separate DNA fragments?  
**Back:**
1. Put DNA samples into wells at one end of a gel.
2. Apply an electric current.
3. DNA is negatively charged, so it moves towards the **positive electrode**.
4. The gel acts like a sieve: **smaller fragments move faster/further** than larger ones.
5. Stain or otherwise visualise the DNA to see bands. A DNA size ladder gives fragments of known sizes for comparison.

### F04 — Reading a DNA gel
**Front:** How do you use a DNA gel to compare samples?  
**Back:**
- Find the **DNA size ladder** (reference bands of known sizes).
- Compare how far each sample's bands travelled with the ladder to estimate fragment sizes.
- Bands at the same position represent fragments of similar length.
- Matching band patterns suggest samples may share a source, but a match alone does not prove identity.

## G. Bioethics — highest priority

### G01 — Three ethical approaches
**Front:** What question does each ethical approach ask?  
**Back:**
- **Consequences-based:** Which choice gives the best overall balance of benefits and harms?
- **Duty/rule-based:** What rights, duties or rules must be respected, whatever the outcome?
- **Virtue-based:** What would a person with good character (e.g. honesty, compassion and responsibility) do?
In an exam, apply the approach to details in the scenario; do not just name it.

### G02 — Ethical principles
**Front:** What do beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, respect and integrity mean?  
**Back:**
- **Beneficence:** do good; promote wellbeing.
- **Non-maleficence:** avoid or reduce harm.
- **Justice:** be fair; share benefits and risks fairly.
- **Respect:** recognise people's dignity, choices and rights.
- **Integrity:** be honest and act responsibly.

### G03 — Approach vs principle
**Front:** How is an ethical approach different from an ethical principle?  
**Back:** An **approach** is a way to think through a decision (consequences-, duty/rule- or virtue-based). A **principle** is a value used to judge the decision (such as justice or respect). You can use principles when applying an approach.

### G04 — Bioethical scenario method
**Front:** What steps should you follow when answering an unfamiliar bioethical scenario?  
**Back:**
1. State the biological issue or decision.
2. Name the people/groups affected (**stakeholders**).
3. Identify the possible benefits, harms and rights/duties involved.
4. Apply the ethical approach or principle to specific facts in the scenario.
5. Give your judgement and support it with reasons; mention an important counterpoint if relevant.

### G05 — Applying approaches to a scenario
**Front:** A genetic test may help a person make health decisions, but it could reveal private information about relatives. Give one consequences-based point and one duty-based point.  
**Back:**
- **Consequences-based:** weigh the possible health benefit against harms such as worry, discrimination or family conflict for everyone affected.
- **Duty-based:** consider the person's right to **informed consent** and **confidentiality** (keeping private information private).
Link each point to the scenario. Do not just list generic pros and cons.

### G06 — Strong ethical conclusion
**Front:** What makes a bioethical conclusion convincing in an exam?  
**Back:**
- Clearly state your position.
- Support it with relevant biological evidence.
- Explain the ethical reasoning (not just personal opinion).
- Show that you understand a competing concern or limitation.
A good answer explains **why** your choice is justified.
